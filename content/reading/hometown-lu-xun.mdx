In Lu Xun's *Hometown* (故乡), the narrator returns to his village and reunites with Runtu, the spirited boy he adored as a child. He's been carrying that golden memory for years. And then Runtu, now weathered and poor, calls him **"Master."**

## Why that one word lands so hard

As children there was no distance between them — just two boys and a watermelon field under the moon. The word "Master" doesn't insult anyone. It's polite. It's *correct*. And that's the horror of it: the gap between them has become so normal that Runtu reaches for it instinctively, out of respect.

The cruelty isn't an act. It's a structure. Class, time, and circumstance have done the wounding long before this moment; the word just makes the wound visible.

## What stayed with me

- A single word can carry the full weight of everything that's changed.
- Nostalgia is partly grief for a self, and a closeness, that the world quietly took.
- The narrator's hope at the end — that the next generation won't be walled off like this — is fragile, and all the more moving for it.

> Hope is like a road: it isn't there until enough people walk it.

I think about that "Master" more than almost anything else I've read this year.
