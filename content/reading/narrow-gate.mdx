Gide's *La Porte étroite* is about two people who love each other and a religious ideal that quietly devours it. Alissa renounces earthly love to pursue something higher — and the novel never lets us be sure whether that's holiness or the most elaborate self-deception imaginable.

## What unsettled me

The cruelty isn't loud. No one is villainous. Alissa's renunciation is gentle, reasoned, even beautiful — which is exactly what makes it terrifying. She talks herself out of happiness one tender, principled step at a time.

## The question I keep turning over

Where is the line between **restraint** and **fear dressed up as restraint**? Between choosing a higher good and being too afraid of an ordinary one?

- Is her virtue a genuine calling, or an excuse to avoid the vulnerability of being loved?
- Does naming something "sacred" make it immune to honest examination?
- How often do I do a smaller version of this — refusing a good thing and calling the refusal maturity?

> The most dangerous self-deceptions are the ones that feel like integrity.

A short book that left a long shadow.
